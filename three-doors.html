A capital primer for private club boards

The Three Doors: Sell, Raise, or Reprice

Every private club is a depreciating asset funded by a membership that must be continuously re-sold. Sooner or later the capital bill arrives, and it exits through one of three doors. This primer shows how each door actually works, with named clubs, real votes, verified numbers, and the models a board can run with its own figures.

The Thesis

A board cannot choose whether the club is underwritten. Only who does the underwriting.

Wherever real demand exists for a club, three judges price it: the buyer if it sells, the lender and the membership if it raises capital, and the prospective member every single year regardless. Deferred capital does not disappear. It moves between these three prices until someone pays it.

Clubs rarely die at a single vote. They erode by sequence: debt taken at the top of a cycle, a shrinking and aging roster, assessments that recur until they read as a distress signal, and then a sale vote that passes almost unanimously, because by then selling is the relief.

Capital Positions

Your capital position is measured, not felt.

Depreciation is the future arriving on schedule. The industry's own benchmarking says most clubs generate less capital than they consume, and the club that feels fine is often the one quietly consuming its own asset base.

Investing

Capital income exceeds depreciation.

The physical plant is renewed as fast as it wears. Member value compounds, and the club walks into any examination prepared.

Coasting

Capital income trails depreciation.

Nothing looks wrong yet. The club is consuming its asset base in place, and the gap accrues silently as next decade's backlog.

Starved

The deferred bill has come due.

Capital needs now exceed what the membership can or will fund. From here, someone else sets the price.

Primary framework

The benchmarks a finance committee can adopt tomorrow.

  • Bare minimum: generate capital income at least equal to annual depreciation before funding aspirational projects. Club Benchmarking, March 2022.9
  • Sustainability floor: a Net Capital Available Ratio (net capital available divided by operating revenue) of approximately 12 percent. The fiscal 2014 industry median was 11 percent, meaning the median club sat below the line. Club Benchmarking, June 2015.10
  • Current recommended target: Club Benchmarking's more recent Capital KPIs guidance points to roughly 17 percent, based on its database of over 1,000 clubs. Publication date of the 17 percent figure needs confirmation before quoting it in a board packet.10

Use reserve-study economics, not book depreciation, and measure over a rolling five-to-ten-year window. A snapshot taken the year after a renovation will misclassify a healthy club.

The Three Judges

Same club. Three prices. One deferred bill.

The buyer and the lender are dormant judges who wake when summoned. The prospective member is a sitting judge who rules every year, because attrition means every club is perpetually selling memberships whether the board notices or not.

Door One

Sell: the vote is nearly unanimous by the time it happens.

The most misunderstood fact about clubs that sell is how the members felt on the day of the vote. Not defeated. Relieved. Four cases, four landslides.

The sequence that ends in a landslide.

No club sells because of one bad year. The pattern in the record is a sequence, and a board can locate itself on it years before a buyer does.

1. Boom-era debt

White Manor borrowed roughly $6M for a 2003 course renovation. Blue Hill borrowed about $6M for a 2012 clubhouse project. Both loans were taken in confident years.

2. The roster thins

Membership decline shrinks the base that must carry the debt. Blue Hill's decline is company-reported; Wisconsin's City Club lost roughly 300 members during COVID per BizTimes.

3. Capital talk turns toxic

Recurring assessments and stalled projects poison the room. At Blue Hill, a board member said capital discussions "got so contentious that we never even voted." White Manor entered its final year deferring roughly $317,000 of planned capital projects on $155,000 of operating income (company-reported).

4. The relief vote

A buyer offers to retire the debt, fund the projects, and end the assessments. The vote passes in a landslide, because by then selling feels like rescue.

Corroborated

What the buyer is actually screening for.

Concert Golf Partners, the most active recapitalizer of member-owned clubs, publishes its own criteria: existing debt that blocks reinvestment, and member assessments that have become recurring. Its model is to retire all club debt, fund capital improvements, and take over management, in exchange for ownership. All three claims verified 3-0 against its published materials.

Read that carefully: the funding mechanism boards reach for most readily, the recurring assessment, is simultaneously the signal that puts a club on an acquirer's list.

Corroborated

What members argue about once it is too late.

At White Manor, a bondholder objected that the roughly $46,000 per acre sale price undervalued land worth about $88,000 per acre nearby, and urged delaying the vote to explore local ownership. The sale closed anyway.

The lesson is not that he was right about the price. It is that by the time a club reaches the sale table, members are negotiating the terms of the exit rather than the direction of the club.

"It ends our debt challenges and ends our longstanding internal debates about funding capital projects." Jim Marano, club president, Blue Hill Country Club, on the sale. Reported independently, 2016.
The honest counter-case: Oakland Hills. In July 2016 the membership rejected an $11M dues-funded course restoration. The club did not spiral toward a sale. The board revised the plan, won a 79 percent yes vote on a roughly $12M version, delivered the course in 2021, and the same membership later approved a clubhouse rebuild that grew past $104M after the 2022 fire. Still member-owned. A failed vote is a warning marker on the sequence, not a death sentence. What separates Oakland Hills from White Manor is demand depth, member wealth, and a board that revised and returned instead of assessing and retreating. All independently reported by Crain's Detroit Business and The Detroit News.

Board checklist

Reading an unsolicited offer.

  • Separate the three components: debt retired, capex committed, and consideration to members. The first two are the buyer paying your deferred bill; only the third is new value.
  • Ask what contractual protections survive closing: use covenants (Capital City secured a golf-course requirement), legacy member rates, and for how long. White Manor's golf commitment was reported at seven years.
  • Get the land appraised independently before the vote, not after. The White Manor dissent came too late to matter.
  • Price the alternative honestly: run the model above and put the monthly self-funding number next to the offer, so members choose with both prices visible.
2

Door Two

Raise: the funding stack is the strategy.

Staying member-owned is a financing discipline, not a sentiment. The clubs that hold the door shut fund capital structurally, so no single project ever requires the vote that breaks the room.

Primary framework

Dues pay the bills.

GGA Partners, January 2025: monthly dues should be used to pay the club's bills. Operations, not capital. A dues increase cannot fix a capital problem.

Primary framework

Joining fees fund the machine.

Joining fees paid by incoming members fund capital asset replacements first. Club Benchmarking notes initiation fees are the most common capital income source, and their volatility is why many clubs struggle.

Primary framework

Debt and assessments are the fallbacks.

When joining fees are inadequate, clubs turn to debt, capital calls on members, or a combination. And recurring assessments are precisely what acquirers screen for.

Primary framework

How member-owned clubs actually fund capital right now.

The GGA Partners and National Club Association board study (reported November 2025): 66 percent of clubs fund capital through entrance fees, 65 percent through capital dues, 50 percent through special assessments, and 40 percent are willing to use debt. 71 percent require a formal member vote for capital projects. The discipline gap: 61 percent have a capital reserve study, but only 35 percent have a current strategic plan, reserve study, and facility master plan together, and 60 percent either have no capital reserve or share it with the maintenance budget.

The tools are common. The discipline is rare. A board that simply maintains all three documents and walls off its reserve is already operating ahead of roughly two thirds of its peers.

Corroborated

The vote playbook, from the club that failed one and then didn't.

Oakland Hills rejected an $11M restoration in July 2016. The plan asked members to fund it through increased dues and close the South Course for 14 months. The board did not retreat into assessments or deferral. It revised scope and funding, returned, and won 79 percent approval for a roughly $12M plan. The delivered course reopened in 2021, and delivery became the collateral for every vote since, up to and including a nine-figure clubhouse rebuild.

  • Failed votes are usually funding-structure votes, not project votes. The membership did not hate the restoration; it declined that particular way of paying for it.
  • Revise and return beats defer and decay. The second vote came with tightened scope and a clearer case.
  • Delivered projects compound trust. Each completed project made the next, larger vote easier, the exact opposite of the erosion sequence behind Door 1.

Board checklist

Sequencing a capital campaign that survives the vote.

  • Adopt the funding stack formally before proposing any project: dues for operations, joining fees and capital dues for replacement capital, debt sized to survive a downturn, assessments reserved for true one-time events.
  • Publish the capital health readout annually. A membership that has watched the gap for three years does not need convincing in year four.
  • Phase visibly: deliver a small project, then ask for the bigger one. Oakland Hills' 79 percent came after revision; its nine-figure votes came after delivery.
  • Never let an assessment recur. Once it becomes annual, it reads as distress to members and buyers alike.

Door Three

Reprice: the prospect is the judge who never adjourns.

Attrition means every club sells memberships every year. Initiation and dues are set by what the next member will pay, and the next member tours the physical plant before reading the fee schedule. Amenity condition is the collateral behind the price.

$29K to $50K Median US club initiation fee, 2019 to 2022, a 72 percent rise. Club Benchmarking data via Front Office Sports, August 2025. A second series attributed to Club + Resort Business puts 2019 at $25K reaching $50K by 2024; both converge at about $50K today.

Corroborated

The escalation era is measured, and it is decelerating.

GGA Partners' August 2024 survey with CMAA: initiation fees rose 8.7 percent on average (average fee $58K, median $34K) and operating dues 6.2 percent, both decelerating from the prior year. At the elite end the anecdotes run far ahead of the medians: La Gorce at a reported $1 million initiation with a years-long waitlist (Fortune, August 2024) and Apogee at up to $650,000 (Front Office Sports, August 2025). NGF reports private club membership up roughly 50 percent since 2019 with facilities approaching capacity.

The deceleration is the strategic point. When 9 percent increases fade to a 5 percent normal and waitlist share drifts down from its peak, pricing power stops being cycle-wide and starts being earned club by club. The next increase gets defended by collateral, and the collateral is the capital plan.

The mechanism

What the club actually sells is future dues streams.

A membership sold to a 45-year-old household is a decades-long dues stream plus a family's activity. The same membership sold to a 68-year-old is a fraction of that. The age of the incoming cohort is the club's real revenue forecast, and amenity mix is the strongest lever a board holds over who applies.

The claim that racquets shifts the incoming cohort younger is an inference until your club measures it. The model below shows why it is worth measuring.

Board checklist

Raising fees without spending trust.

  • Sequence matters: visible investment first, or announced simultaneously. A fee increase against a tired plant reads as extraction; against a live capital plan it reads as positioning.
  • Track waitlist depth and applicant age as standing board metrics. They are your pricing power and your future dues base, respectively.
  • Tie the increase letter to the capital plan explicitly: what was delivered, what is next, what the increase protects. Members accept prices they can see the collateral behind.
  • Watch the cycle. Fee power built in a demand boom evaporates fastest at clubs whose amenities were coasting through it.

The Racquets Lens

Where racquet sports actually fit, door by door.

Not "the highest engagement per dollar." That claim does not survive scrutiny without club-level data. What survives: courts are among the cheapest visible, high-frequency engagement builders a club can add, and the acquirers repositioning purchased clubs are adding them.

Door 1

Buyers build what boards deferred.

Concert Golf named pickleball among planned investments at Wisconsin Country Club (company-reported), and its Fountains buildout centered a 12-court racquets complex (company-reported). PE repositioning playbooks are a revealed preference: the buyer spends on visible engagement first.

Door 2

Small, phaseable, and visible wins votes.

Court projects cost a fraction of clubhouse work, phase naturally, and every member walks past them. One tennis court converts to four pickleball courts (BoardRoom Magazine, 2025). That is the profile of capital that passes: modest ask, visible delivery, trust compounded for the next vote.

Door 3

Household reach is the recruiting story.

Racquets serves spouses, juniors, and social members golf alone does not reach. Whether it shifts your applicant age is measurable within a season of programming. Measure it, then let the cohort model price it.

Primary framework

The demand signal is already inside the survey data.

In GGA's August 2024 survey, club leaders named pickleball the second most over-capacity amenity at 41 percent, behind only golf at 50 percent, and pickleball sits among the top planned capital projects at 28 percent of clubs. SFIA counted 19.8 million US pickleball players in 2024, up 46 percent in a year. National numbers justify studying your club's demand. They never substitute for it.

Corroborated

Courts are being run as revenue lines, not just amenities.

Turnberry Isle (Aventura FL) opened three pickleball and two padel courts in December 2025 with paid, pre-booked access: $40 per 90-minute reservation, $15 per hour guest rate, lessons at $35 per half hour. Club + Resort Business and the operator's release, December 2025. For a board, that is a working template for court programming that carries its own operating math.

Read this before believing any of it

Who benefits if your board believes this primer.

Court builders profit when clubs build courts, including the company behind this resource. Private equity buyers profit when boards conclude member ownership cannot fund capital. Consultants profit from the studies in between. None of that makes the evidence above false, but it means a board should discount every recommendation by its source's interest, this one included.

The protection is procedural: demand utilization data from comparable clubs rather than national participation statistics, take multiple bids, phase commitments, and measure demand at your own club before building anything. Advice that survives those four filters is worth acting on regardless of who offered it.

Falsification

What would prove this primer wrong.

A thesis that cannot name its own kill conditions is marketing. These are the tripwires a board should watch.

On capital positions

If clubs persistently below the capital-income-versus-depreciation line show no deterioration in member metrics or asset condition over ten years, the framework fails. Watch reserve studies against condition audits.

On the three judges

If fee attainment and sale outcomes show no relationship to amenity condition across a full demand cycle, the underwriting lens fails. The test arrives when the current waitlist era ends, not before.

On the erosion sequence

If most clubs that fail a major capital vote remain healthy and member-owned five years later, the sequence overstates the risk. Oakland Hills is the live counter-example; the base rate is the open question.

On racquets

If court reservation data 24 months after a build shows low sustained utilization, or the applicant age mix does not move, the recruitment claim is dead at that club regardless of national statistics.

Board Memo

The one-page version for the board packet.

Copy it, put your club's numbers in the diagnostic, and attach both.

A private club is a depreciating asset funded by a membership that must be continuously re-sold. Wherever demand for the club is real, three judges price it: a buyer if it sells, the lender and the membership if it raises capital, and the prospective member every year regardless. The board cannot choose whether the club is underwritten, only who does the underwriting and how prepared the club is when it happens.

Our capital position is measurable. The industry benchmark is simple: capital income should at least equal annual depreciation, and net capital available should reach roughly 12 percent of operating revenue as a floor, with current guidance pointing meaningfully higher. The industry median has historically sat below the floor. We should assume we are the median until our own numbers prove otherwise, and we should publish that readout to the finance committee annually.

The record on clubs that sold is consistent. The sequence was debt taken in confident years, a thinning roster, capital discussions that turned contentious or stopped happening, assessments that became recurring, and then a sale vote that passed in a landslide: 167 to 9 in Tallahassee in 2026, near-unanimous outcomes in Milwaukee, Canton, and Malvern before it. Members did not lose those clubs in the sale vote. They lost them years earlier, one deferred project at a time. The acquirers say plainly what they screen for: debt that blocks reinvestment and assessments that recur.

The record also shows the alternative. Oakland Hills failed an eleven million dollar vote in 2016, revised the plan, passed twelve million at 79 percent, delivered, and has since funded over one hundred million more, still member-owned. Failed votes are survivable. Unstructured capital funding is what kills.

Our funding stack should therefore be formal policy: dues fund operations, joining fees and capital dues fund replacement capital, debt is sized to survive a downturn, and assessments are reserved for true one-time events, never recurring. Amenity investment, racquets included, should be evaluated as what it is: among the cheapest visible engagement capital available, with demand measured at our club before we commit, phased so each delivery builds trust for the next vote, and judged by whether it moves utilization, retention, and the age of our incoming cohort.
